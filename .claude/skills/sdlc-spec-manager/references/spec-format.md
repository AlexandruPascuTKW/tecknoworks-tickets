# Spec format

A spec states what a domain **is**: its vocabulary, what people can do and see in it (its actions
and views), and the rules that constrain them. It is read by the product owner, by the team, and
by agents that implement from it — so it stays short, stable and free of anything the code or the
scenarios already say.

Every example below comes from the worked example, `assets/example/specs/LND/lending.md` — a library
lending domain. Read it alongside this file.

## Sections

In this order, and no others unless a domain truly needs one (see the last paragraph):

1. `## Contents` — generated by `scripts/build_toc.py`, never edited by hand. It links every heading
   down to actions and views, and badges them: each action or view shows 🟢/🟡/🔴, ❓ when the
   entry itself needs PO clarification, and "❓ via LND-R…" naming the cited rules that do; each
   rule or field group shows ❓ and a count. Re-run it after every change.
2. `## Context` — why the domain exists and whose problem it solves, in the business's own terms:
   the real situation, not a statement of purpose. A few paragraphs.
3. `## Glossary`
4. `## Use Cases` — the actions and views
5. `## Fields`
6. `## Domain Rules`

Use Cases and Domain Rules carry the spec; Context and Glossary make them readable. A new
section is justified only when it answers a question none of these can hold — the likely one is a
`## States` section once a lifecycle with named states and transitions is shared by several
actions. Grouping with `###` is not a new section.

## Glossary

Split into `###` groups so it can be scanned — for example *Core*, *Loans and Holds*, *People and
roles*, *Owned by other specs*. Choose groups that fit the domain. Each group is a
`| Term | Definition |` table.

- **Definitions only.** A definition says what a term *means*. Anything that constrains behaviour —
  "never renewed while overdue", "only a Branch Manager waives it", "kept even when paid" — is a
  rule and goes in Domain Rules. Rules hidden in definitions get missed by implementers and
  duplicated by later edits.
  Not every "never" is a rule, though. If removing the phrase would change what the term *is* —
  "a Loan runs from the moment the Item is lent until it comes back or is declared lost" — it is
  definition and stays. If it states something the product must enforce and someone could get
  wrong — who may do what, what is kept, what is refused, what is recorded — it is a rule. Ask:
  would a developer write a test for this? If yes, it's a rule.
- **Define every domain noun the rules use**, including everyday-sounding ones that carry a
  specific meaning here (e.g. *Item*: one lendable copy — two copies of the same Title are two
  Items).
- **Define a term by what it is, never by the entry that uses it.** "*Renewal*: a renewal made with
  LND-A3" while LND-A3 describes itself as "a renewal" defines neither (KI-23).
- **Short forms in brackets**: `**<Term>** (<short form>)`, then use the short form
  consistently.
- **Disambiguate look-alikes** inside both definitions — e.g. *Lost* (a Loan status) says
  "unrelated to an Item withdrawn from the collection".
- **Roles** are glossary terms too (Member, Librarian, Branch Manager), described by what they do —
  their permissions live on the actions and views (*Who can*).
- **Terms owned by another domain** go in an *Owned by other specs* group naming the owner in plain
  text (`Owned by the Membership spec (MEM-001)`). No links to other spec files.
- **Defining terms ahead of their actions is fine** while a spec is being built a stage at a
  time — the glossary describes the whole domain's vocabulary. Check every term is used only once
  the spec is complete.
- **Give glossary groups names that no other group uses** — glossary, use-case, field and rule groups
  share one heading namespace, and a duplicate heading gets a `-1` anchor that breaks its Contents
  link (KI-18).

## Use Cases

What people can do and see in the domain. Each use case is exactly one of two kinds, and the spec
keeps the distinction — in its IDs (`LND-A…`, `LND-V…`), its *Input* or *Returns* line, and the
section intro:

| Kind | What it is | Examples |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Action** | Something a person does that changes something in the domain — afterwards, a record is different | Lend an Item · Place a Hold · Waive a Fine |
| **View** | Something a person looks up to decide what to do — nothing changes by looking | Find a Member's Loans · See a Title's Hold queue |

Developers may know these as commands and queries; the spec says action and view because the
product owner reads it.

Anything that is **neither** belongs in Domain Rules, not here:

- what the system does on its own — raising a Fine on a late return, setting an Item aside for the
  next Hold;
- what is derived — a Loan's status, a Member's outstanding total.

The test: *does a person do or look at this?* If not, it is a rule. A blended entry ("Handle a
late return") usually hides all three — a rule (the Fine is raised), a view (seeing what is owed)
and an action (waiving it); split it.

Open the section by stating its conventions once — who can (`Who can lists everyone allowed; anyone
not listed cannot.`), the scope rule, the build status, and the clarification and blocker markers —
then group entries under `###` headings by **stage of the domain's life** (e.g. *Lending, renewing
and ending a Loan*, *Holding a Title*, *Settling Fines*), each with a one-line description. A group's
name must match everything in it — not one part of a lifecycle while other groups hold the rest,
not a mix of a specific action and tools every action uses (KI-22). Each entry is a `####` under its
group:

```markdown
### Lending, renewing and ending a Loan

Lending an Item, renewing its Loan, and ending the Loan when the Item comes back or is lost.

#### LND-A1 — Lend an Item

Lend an Item to a Member, starting a Loan.

- **Who can:** Librarian, Branch Manager
- **Input:** Member (required), Item (required)
- **Rules:** LND-R4, LND-R5, LND-R6, LND-R15
- **Status:** 🟢 Built
```

- **Input** (actions) and **Returns** (views) are written in domain names, not IDs, so the
  product owner can read them. Name a **whole concept** when the action takes or the view returns
  all of it — `Input: a Hold`, `Returns: the whole Loan`. Name the **fields** when it uses only
  some, exactly as the Fields table writes them, marking what the action requires:
  `Input: Title (required), Pickup branch (required)`. Use `—` when it acts on the record alone.
- **Returns** adds any derived values (a Loan's status, an outstanding total). A view may add
  **Can filter by**. Name *what* is returned, never layout.

- **One business decision per action.** "Renew a Loan" is one action whether it is renewed at the
  counter or by the Member; "Cancel a Hold" covers a *Waiting* and a *Ready* Hold alike. Split only
  when the decision, the actor or the rules differ (renewing a Loan within its limits vs a Branch
  Manager extending a Due date past them).
- **Business names, not technical ones.** Name it the way the business would say it. No payloads,
  types, endpoints or error codes.
- **Who can is the only home of a permission.** List everyone allowed; "anyone not listed cannot"
  covers the rest. Where one role can do everything another can (a Branch Manager does everything a
  Librarian does), list both on every entry. An inferred permission is marked
  *[❓ Needs PO clarification]* on the entry.
- **Status is 🟢 Built, 🟡 Partly built or 🔴 Not built**, judged against the code by
  `sdlc-build-status` — never set by hand or from intent:
  - **🟢 Built** — a user can do it end to end, and everything the entry states conforms: *Who can* (enforced
    server-side), *Input*, every rule on its *Rules* line.
  - **🟡 Partly built** — a user can do it end to end, but some of what it states doesn't conform yet. The entry
    then carries a **Gaps** line naming exactly which rule or field IDs aren't met, right under
    *Status*: `- **Gaps:** LND-R8`. The Gaps line is what keeps 🟡 honest: no Gaps, no 🟡.
    Right under Gaps goes a **To reach 🟢** line: one line of prose, in the spec's own business
    words, saying what has to change for those gaps to be met — `- **To reach 🟢:** raise a Fine
    when an Overdue Loan's Item comes back (LND-R8).` The spec is read by the product owner and the
    business, so it names no files, endpoints or code. Only a 🟡 entry carries it, and every 🟡
    entry does.
  - **🔴 Not built** — a user can't do it yet. An endpoint nothing calls, or a screen on mock data,
    is still 🔴.

  When something ships in parts because it is really two decisions, split the action instead.
  Scenarios stay 🟢/🔴: a scenario either passes or it doesn't.
- **Views have a Who can too** — seeing information is scoped like doing things.
- **Data scope is one rule, stated once.** *Who can* names roles; which records a user may touch is
  a scope rule (LND-R1: staff act within their own Branch, Members on their own records). The
  section intro says every action and view applies only within that scope, so no entry repeats
  it — and a rule must never grant scope only to one action.
- **What refuses an action lives in one rule**, as a list (LND-R4: what refuses a loan). Other rules
  and actions point to it rather than keeping their own lists, which drift.
- **Group, don't nest.** No root entry with sub-entries, no sub-numbers (LND-A1.1). Flat numbers
  are addresses; the group headings do the organising, so a group may list LND-A7 after LND-A12.
- **Name the business action, not the screen.** Groups follow what the user gets done, not the
  tabs of the current UI — screens change, actions don't.
- **The Rules line lists the rules that shape this entry**: the ones someone building only
  this entry would get wrong without reading. That means any rule that names the entry, its action
  or its object. Two kinds stay off the line:
  - **Background rules** describe the model and no entry enforces them ("time alone never closes a
    Loan", LND-R2). They stand alone; `check_refs.py` lists them as "fine if intended".
  - **Rules shared by every action of a kind** (what every action checks, records,
    all-or-nothing) are named by one umbrella rule, and each entry cites only that rule. This is the
    same as "point to the owning rule" for lists.

  A line of more than 12 ids, or ids that repeat on most entries, means the line is padded (KI-19).
- **Rule ids are listed explicitly**: `LND-R1, LND-R2, LND-R3`, never `LND-R1–R3`. Ranges break as
  soon as rules are added or reused, and an explicit list can be searched for.
- **No steps or screens** — those are scenario material.

## Fields

The fields of the domain's concepts — every piece of information an action takes or a view
returns — each **defined once** with what makes a value valid. Validation is a product requirement:
the database, domain model, application layer and front end all enforce the same definition and
cite its ID (e.g. `// LND-F5`), so a change is one edit.

**Group fields by concept.** Each `###` heading is a concept the Glossary defines — the thing
that is recorded, changed and shown as a unit (*Loan*, *Hold*, *Fine*). What the concept always
needs is one sentence under its heading ("A Hold always has a Member, a Title, a Pickup branch and
the time it was placed"). A group that isn't a concept — the details people give when they decide
something — says so in its opening line.

```markdown
### Loan

A Loan always has a Member, an Item, a Lending branch, a Loaned on date and a Due date.

| ID | Field | Allowed values and validation | Source column |
|----|-------|-------------------------------|---------------|
| **LND-F5** | Due date | A calendar date after Loaned on (LND-R6, LND-R11) | `due_date` |
| **LND-F9** | Renewal count | A whole number from 0 to 2 (LND-R10) | `renewals` |
```

- **A field defines only what makes a value valid** — kind of value, allowed values or list,
  ranges, formats, or another concept ("a Title in the Catalogue"). No *Required* column: whether
  a field is needed depends on where it is used.
- **Requiredness lives in four places:** an action's *Input*; the rule listing what must hold
  before a key transition (in the example, LND-R4 for lending); cross-field rules for conditional
  cases ("a Collect by only while the Hold is *Ready*"); and the concept's own sentence for what it
  can never lack.
- **Rules that span fields stay in Domain Rules** and cite the IDs; don't restate a field's
  validation in a rule.
- **Value lists that change** (Loan periods, fine rates) are written as "one of the configured
  list", not hard-coded; the list itself is reference data.
- **A source column is optional.** Where values arrive from somewhere with its own column names —
  an import template, a legacy export — a last column maps each field to it, since that source
  fills the same fields. Leave the column out when there is no such source.
- **Fields another spec also uses** are defined by the spec that owns the concept; other specs cite
  its IDs. There is no shared catalogue — each spec owns its model.
- **No types, lengths or database names** — those are technical, derived in the build.

## Domain Rules

```markdown
### Holds

**LND-R14 — First placed, first met.** A Hold queue is met in the order its Holds were placed (Placed
on). A Member has at most one Hold on a Title that is *Waiting* or *Ready*.
```

- Group rules under `###` by stage or topic (*Scope and Loan status*, *Loans*, *Holds*, *Fines*).
  Put the rules every action shares first (scope, the lifecycle, the action contract), then one
  group per use-case group, **in the same order**, so a reader moves between the two sections
  without searching (KI-22). A rule may stand on its own — rules written ahead of their action are
  a normal part of building iteratively, not a defect.
- **Write the outcome someone can observe, not the mechanism or the source's jargon.** "A Member
  gets the same answer at every Branch", not "all Branches read one shared policy store" (KI-14).
- **A rule never grants a permission.** "A Branch Manager can reverse a Fine" in a rule leaves the
  actions — where "anyone not listed cannot" — saying nobody can. Put every *who may* on the
  action's *Who can* and keep the rule to *what happens*.
- A rule must constrain something someone could get wrong. "A Loan has a status" is not a rule.
- Rules point the other way too: when a rule leans on another, name it (`the Item goes on as in
  LND-R15`).

### What an action needs

What an action takes is its *Input* line, and each field's validity is under Fields —
neither is a rule. Write a rule only for what spans fields or actions: what must hold before a key
transition (LND-R4: what refuses a loan) and conditional requirements ("a Collect by whenever a
Hold is *Ready*").

## IDs

| Kind | Form | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| Action | `<DOM>-An` | LND-A1 |
| View | `<DOM>-Vn` | LND-V1 |
| Domain rule | `<DOM>-Rn` | LND-R4 |
| Field | `<DOM>-Fn` | LND-F5 |

A project already numbered with other letters for actions and views (say `C` and `Q`) keeps them by
setting `use_case_letters` in its `spec-config.json` (see `templates.md`); R and F are fixed.

Field IDs are for code, tests and other specs, which cite `LND-F5`, not "Due date": names get
refined and collide across specs (every domain has a *Status*), IDs do neither. Inside the spec,
*Input* and *Returns* use the names, so each name must be unique within its spec.

`<DOM>` is the spec's domain code — two to five capital letters (LND, MEM, PAY…). Always write the
full ID, even inside the spec itself: IDs are unique across the project, so code, tests, scenarios
and other specs can cite them unambiguously. Search them with word boundaries (`grep -w LND-R1`),
since `LND-R1` is a prefix of `LND-R12`. Another spec is cited by its own code, such as `MEM-001`
(the scripts' `cross_spec_id_pattern` says how your project writes it).

Numbered per spec file. **Never reused or renumbered** once the file is committed — scenarios,
tests and conversations cite them. A withdrawn rule is struck through with a date and reason:
`~~**LND-R13 — …**~~ [WITHDRAWN 2026-09-02: reason]`. Before the first commit, renumber freely so
numbers run in order within their groups.

After the first commit, new rules take the next free number and go in the group where they
belong, even if that puts LND-R21 above LND-R14. Logical grouping matters more to a reader than
numeric order; the ID is only an address.

## Marking what isn't settled

One marker, inline in italics, right under the rule, entry or term it qualifies:

`*[❓ Needs PO clarification — …]*`

It means the product owner has not yet clarified this point; everything unmarked is clarified.
The markers are the questions the product owner answers: after the dash, **ask a direct question
they can answer on their own**, then say what the working answer was inferred from or that it is
pending a check, and cite the open question that already asks it, if there is one (`— may Members
renew their own Loans, or only staff at the counter? Inferred from the self-service renewals the
current system offers.` / `… Open question 3.`). A reader can then tell "built on an assumption"
from "no position yet". Open questions live in the spec's `open-questions.md`, kept with
`sdlc-open-questions`.

- **Write the working answer as the rule.** If the sources hold a working conclusion, an agreed
  simplification or a proposed position — even one still awaiting confirmation — state it as the
  rule and add the marker; don't leave the rule out. Re-check the conversation and the sources for
  one before writing "no position yet": flagging an agreed position as open re-opens a decision
  already made.
- **Only the product owner's clarification removes the marker** — whoever they consulted. Our own
  inference never does.
- **One marker per unsettled point — add, never overwrite.** When a new question comes up on a rule
  that already has a marker (for example during implementation), add a second marker beside it.
  Replacing the existing one silently drops a question nobody has answered.

### Blockers

A few open points shape the foundation of the build: answering them wrongly would mean reworking
the data model, the access model, or whether a whole workflow exists — for example, where a role is
held, whether one record can have several of another, or whether a Fine can be waived in part.
Mark those with a blocker in front of the usual marker:

`*[⛔ Blocker — ❓ Needs PO clarification — …]*`

- **The ❓ stays inside.** A blocker is still a clarification, so everything above applies, and the
  scripts count it as one.
- **Keep it rare** — a handful per spec at most. Importance alone doesn't make a blocker; the test
  is whether a wrong answer would force rework of what's already built. Everything else stays ❓.
- **Say what ⛔ means once**, in the Use Cases intro, so the product owner can read it (see the
  template).
- **Contents shows it:** `build_toc.py` badges an entry "⛔ via …" when it cites a blocked rule,
  and a rule or field group "⛔ n".
- **A blocker clears like any marker** — only the product owner's answer removes it.

Don't attribute rules to people in the spec; it states what is true, not who said it.

## Each fact once

A fact lives in exactly one place. The usual leaks:

- a permission in a rule or a glossary definition (belongs on the action's or view's *Who can*);
- a rule in a glossary definition (belongs in Domain Rules);
- the same constraint in Context, a definition and a rule — keep the rule; Context may summarise
  principles in prose but shouldn't be the only home of a rule.

When a rule fits under two `###` groups, either it is duplicated or the grouping is wrong.

## What never goes in a spec

| Content | Where it goes |
|---------|--------------|
| Screens, layout, labels, message wording, routes, column names and types | The code; scenarios for labels |
| Step-by-step flows, acceptance criteria, test cases | Scenarios |
| API contracts, schemas, data models, technology choices | A technical spec or ADR |
| Phases, releases, "in this increment", what's deferred | The backlog |
| Open questions, risks, assumptions sections | Outside the spec; an undecided *rule* is marked inline |
| Links to other spec files | Plain-text spec names or codes only |

The test for anything borderline: could a competent implementer, given this spec and the codebase,
arrive at it without being told? If yes, it's noise.

## The audience rule

A spec is often read by people outside the team — the client, the product owner's colleagues. If
your project has material they must never see cited (internal notes, a private folder, another
repository), list those paths in the project's `spec-config.json` under `forbidden_paths`;
`check_refs.py` then flags every spec line that cites one. Projects without such material leave the
key out.
